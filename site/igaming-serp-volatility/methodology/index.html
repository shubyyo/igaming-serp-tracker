iGaming SERP Volatility Index · Methodology

How the index works

Every step from a Google results page to the number on the badge, including what the index can't tell you.

Gambling keywords are some of the most contested results in search. Casino and sportsbook SERPs churn faster than most verticals, and they're a favorite target for parasite pages and spam. General volatility trackers mix them in with recipes and software reviews. This index looks only at gambling SERPs, one market at a time, and makes every step of the calculation public.

What is measured

Each market has a fixed basket of 50 casino, betting and bonus keywords. Every keyword is fetched once each morning (UTC) from Google's mobile results in that market's main language. We keep the top 10 organic results. Ads, maps and other SERP features don't count.

Baskets were built from Ahrefs keyword data: commercial searches that affiliates and operators compete for, with one keyword per group of near-duplicates. Rules and how-to searches, calculators, brand names and news queries are left out. A few offshore and unlicensed terms are kept on purpose, because that is where parasite pages concentrate.

The baskets are frozen. If a keyword ever has to change, the change is versioned and noted on the chart, because a different basket isn't comparable.

From SERPs to one number

Each result is identified by its site: the host name, lowercased, without www. Google now hides many destination URLs behind its own redirect links, so the host is the part every data provider reports reliably. If one site holds two positions, the second is counted separately (site.com#2), so all ten positions keep their own identity. Then we compare today's list with the keyword's recent consensus: each site's average position over its last 3 verified days, counting a day it was absent as position 11.

Why a consensus and not just yesterday: mobile results for these searches often flip between a few result sets from one fetch to the next, so a single day is a noisy baseline. Against a 3-day consensus, the day-to-day movements of different markets line up more often: on a seven-week pre-launch sample, their correlation rose from 0.26 to 0.35. A real Google change hits many markets at once, while fetch noise doesn't, so this means more signal and less noise. The cost is that a lasting change registers over a few days rather than all on day one.

This means a site swapping one of its pages for another doesn't register as movement; a new site entering the top 10, or sites changing places, does.

Each site that moved contributes its rank change, weighted by where it sits:

E(site)   = mean rank over the last 3 verified days   (absent = position 11)
weight(r) = 1 / log2(1 + r)        r = the better of E(site) and today's rank
D         = Σ weight(r) × |rank_today − E(site)|        (absent today = position 11)
v         = D / Dmax               Dmax = the value for a completely replaced top 10

A swap at positions 1–2 counts far more than a swap at 9–10, which matches how traffic is split. v runs from 0 (identical) to 1 (every site replaced).

A market's daily value V is the 5% trimmed mean of v across its basket, scaled to 0–100. Trimming stops one exploding keyword from moving the whole market.

The score you see places V within that market's own recent history. P50, P80, P95 and P99 are percentiles of the previous 90 clean days, frozen for the calendar month. The score runs in straight lines between fixed points: 5 at P50, 7 at P80, 9 at P95 and 10 at P99 or above. It reaches 0 as far below the median as P99 is above it. A median day therefore scores 5, and the score and the band always agree:

BandV is…
Calmbelow the market's median (P50): score under 5
Normalbetween P50 and P80: 5 to 7
Elevatedbetween P80 and P95: 7 to 9

Each market is scored against itself, so a 7 in Brazil and a 7 in Germany both mean "unusually busy for that market". They don't mean the same number of URLs moved. The all-markets index is the equal-weighted mean of the market values, with its own reference.

Until a series has 30 clean days of its own, it is scored against a starting reference. That reference was measured before launch, on about seven weeks of daily mobile SERPs (2 October to 22 November 2026) for a separate sample of casino and betting keywords in each market. It used the same identities, guard and scoring as the live index. Germany and Spain didn't have enough of that sample, so they use the all-markets starting reference instead. Either way the chart says so, and the series switches to its own trailing reference as soon as it has the history.

Suspected poisoned SERPs

Sometimes a fetch returns a page that doesn't look like the keyword's normal results: a burst of forum threads, a set of unfamiliar domains, or an almost empty page after legal removals. Counting those as volatility would turn a data problem into a fake "storm". Each fetch therefore goes through three stages.

  1. Provider check. When the data comes from DataForSEO, its resolve_poisoned_results option is on, and any result it repaired is marked in our data. SerpApi has no equivalent, so only the next two stages apply to its data.
  2. History check. We collect every host that appeared for the keyword in the last 7 days, counting the keyword's verified days and any fetch from that week. A fetch is suspected when at least 70% of its hosts are new to that set, or when it shows 3 or more Reddit, Quora or social results for a keyword that normally shows at most 1. One exception: a fetch that closely matches the previous verified day (Jaccard ≥ 0.7) is never suspected, because it's a continuation, not a jump. Mobile gambling SERPs change a lot from day to day, and often switch between a few result sets. Being taken over by sites unseen all week is the pattern this check looks for. On the pre-launch sample it flagged about 6% of fetches.
  3. Re-fetch. Each suspected keyword is fetched again about 30 minutes later.
    • If the second fetch looks normal, it's used (recovered).
    • If both fetches look unusual but agree with each other, the result is quarantined. It's shown as provisional (a hollow dot on the chart) and checked against the next day. A next day that matches confirms it as a real shift; otherwise it's discarded.
    • If both look unusual and disagree, the keyword is discarded for that day.

A day stays in the index if at least 80% of its basket is usable once this has settled. Otherwise it's drawn as a gap. Each market's data-quality table shows how many keywords took each path.

These checks infer poisoning from how a result compares with its own history. They don't prove anything about why Google showed it, and nothing here should be read as a statement about Google policy.

Data providers

Results come from a commercial SERP API (SerpApi at launch, DataForSEO later). Every stored result records its provider. On the day the provider changes, keywords are re-baselined rather than scored, so the switch can't show up as volatility, and the chart marks that day.

Known limits

  • One fetch a day from one data center's view of one location. Personalization, local packs and intraday swings aren't captured.
  • Mobile only.
  • There's a warm-up: a keyword's first three days can't be checked against history and aren't scored, because there's no consensus yet. After a change of data provider, each keyword warms up again. The index was published only after a 30-day baseline.
  • Rankings in regulated markets can move for licensing reasons (an operator blocked, a domain change) that have nothing to do with algorithm updates.
  • External events, such as Google updates as reported by industry press, are annotated for context. They aren't inferred from the data.
